# Why bother learning?

Throughout our lives we're told we have to learn things. But as we're educated most of us are never really told *why*. Looking back, the age old line of "so you can get a job" seems deeply dreary for someone who might have hopes and dreams beyond a stuffy corporate desk. For me, any task is much easier if I have a clear sense of why I'm doing it and what I want to get out of it. So here you go.

Fundamentally I think there are three reasons to bother learning things:

1. Learning stuff allows you to do and build cool things (and you should build cool things)
2. Learning and truly understanding things is deeply satisfying in of itself (I'm a huge proponent of nerds)
3. Getting good at learning stuff will help you do and build even cooler things in the future, and more quickly



... looking back on (perhaps too much) education & teaching and trying to actively learn again for the first time in a while, it strikes me that we almost never talk about learning, what the aim is, what the good methods are, how the teaching we're doing hopes to achieve us learning. Implicitly learning must be happening in our education system (at least I hope so), and surely the point of eduation is to enable learning. So why isn't learning *taught*? Why do we just assume it'll happen and that people will pick it up magically along the way? Why do the amazing resources on learning end up as hobby projects and side notes?

... in a world where LLMs can answer any questions we might have nearly instantly is it still worth learning? ...

I don't know why we don't talk about learning more. But what I do know is that you should interesting and deep skills ... and that planning what you do with an understanding of what learning it achieves will help you become much more effective ...

... my goal with this project ... concisely explain what we want to achieve with learning, the stages we need to progress through to get there, and the principles behind what makes good learning ... and how we might be able to learn better, smarter, and quicker in a world where intelligence is readily available and learning becomes optional ...



# The stages of learning

... every topic is different, and there are different things you might want to achieve with your learning, but broadly there are 7 stages ...

0. Identification
   - Before you can go learn you have to have figured out what to learn
   - This is hard in of itself - see Scott Young's Ultra Learning for guidance
   - Even if you're on a course, identifying the most important bits to learn is key

1. Familiarity
   - You have seen it before - a first pass and chance to get to the foot of the learning curve
2. Recall
   - You can remember it (maybe with a small prompt)
3. Understanding
   - You have a conceptual map of how the logic works - can you reconfigure the pieces and identify them jumbled up?
4. Concept matching
   - You can identify where the idea is relevant/useful - can you see the pieces within a new problem, or where they should fit
5. Application
   - You can apply the idea to a situation you haven't seen before and work the logic through - extending the logic beyond the scope you've seen before is the next step
   - More practically this might be that you can cook confidently and make reciepes you've not tried before - there's also a quality aspect the skill which will improve with practice
6. Concept linking
   - You can spot how this concept relates to other concepts and where the points of connection are - which shapes in different concepts are similar? or where might they fit together?
   - In a practical context, you now might realise that [some cooking anaolgy where things are similar], which makes your dinner party a breeze



... recall builds up a store of facts or knowledge, understanding builds up a store of connections (causality) & logic, application builds technical ability, and concept linking builds your fundamental conceptual map of the world ...
