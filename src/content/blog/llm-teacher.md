OUTLINE (from docs/planning.md)

How can we effectively use LLMs to learn? As a teacher, and as a flexible learning tool:
information retrieval, spaced repetition, self-testing, automatic test generation, linked
concept search, understanding validation & feedback, idea validation (my bias or
misunderstanding probably isn't the same as the LLM's), and learning by comparison (think
how you would solve it, get the LLM to solve it, compare, critique, test, update).

Pitfalls to avoid:
- Anti-sycophancy approaches
- Source validation — do you trust where the information is coming from?
- Logic validation — is it actually right? This needs reflection, which is good learning in
  itself.
- The danger of "learning by doing with LLMs": it's much harder and won't happen by
  accident — https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245 ("AI-enhanced productivity is not a shortcut
  to competence")
  - When we let LLMs do large parts of the work we lose the context onto which knowledge is
    built. Facts we come across have nothing to sit on, so they don't stick. Give examples.
  - And using LLMs where we lack context and can't judge quality is riskier — so our
    ability to judge quality in unfamiliar domains is now *super* important. Concept linking
    matters more than ever.

But done well it can be incredible — I've done weeks of statistics research in a morning
with Claude. Get feedback e.g. Neel & Kabla.

"Are we in a post-learning era? No, of course not, but it's suddenly a lot less obvious what
we need to learn." Why self-reflection about our own knowledge now matters so much more — to
search out our knowledge gaps — as does being good at learning quickly, to validate output.

Make sure learning happens at the right level of abstraction for your goals: you don't need
to know how everything works, just how to use the tools at your level. Balance exploitation
with development (doing vs learning) — we need deep technical skills to add value, but we
can't and shouldn't try to be experts in everything.
