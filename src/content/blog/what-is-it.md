OUTLINE (from docs/planning.md)

Most people have a vague concept of learning — but what specifically are we trying to do,
and why? Every topic is different and goals differ, but broadly there are 7 stages:

0. Identification — before you can learn you have to figure out what to learn. Hard in
   itself (see Scott Young's Ultralearning). Even on a course, picking the important bits.
1. Familiarity — you've seen it before; a first pass, the foot of the learning curve
2. Recall — you can remember it (maybe with a small prompt)
3. Understanding — a conceptual map of how the logic works; can you reconfigure the pieces
   and identify them jumbled up?
4. Concept matching — you can spot where the idea is relevant/useful; can you see the
   pieces inside a new problem, or where they should fit?
5. Application — you can apply the idea to a situation you haven't seen and work the logic
   through. Practically: cooking confidently from recipes you've not tried; quality
   improves with practice.
6. Concept linking — you spot how this concept relates to others and where the points of
   connection are. Which shapes in different concepts are similar? Where might they fit
   together? (cooking analogy → dinner party becomes a breeze)

What each stage builds: recall → a store of facts; understanding → connections (causality)
and logic; application → technical ability; concept linking → your fundamental conceptual
map of the world.

Compare to academic theories (details aren't important — they all show the same broad
picture of rising abstraction and deepening skill):
- Bloom's taxonomy — closest to the above; remembering/understanding/applying map almost
  directly onto recall/understanding/application; matching & linking sit roughly where it
  has analysing, though I order matching before application and stop short of evaluating
  and creating. https://www.simplypsychology.org/blooms-taxonomy.html
- Kolb — https://www.simplypsychology.org/learning-kolb.html
- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/epdf/10.3102/00346543060004531 (page 537)
- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00400599211054873
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010028515300657

What do these stages actually let us do? Practical examples — e.g. to spot errors you need
to connect the error log to the underlying logical error (often via similarity to errors
you've seen before). Recall is nearly trivial but matters for communicating with people.
Understanding lets us judge whether facts we don't know are plausible. The ones that really
get things done are concept matching and concept linking — how we figure out what to do and
get new insight about what's possible.
